\begin{abstract}
Several formalisms propose representing a language model's epistemic state with more than one
number: degrees of truth, indeterminacy and falsity as separate components that need not sum to
one. Prior work has shown that models readily emit such triples and that the components
frequently exceed unit mass. What has not been established is whether the elicited triple carries
operationally useful information. We test this directly and under a preregistered decision rule:
holding the prediction target fixed at answer correctness, does an elicited $(T,I,F)$ triple
predict error better than simpler signals obtained from the same model? We compare five signal
families --- the elicited triple, verbalized scalar confidence, self-verification
$P(\text{True})$, sequence log-probability, and discrete semantic entropy --- on four models from
four vendors and four datasets spanning free-form generation (TriviaQA, NQ-open), closed-label
classification with clean labels (SciQ as four-option multiple choice) and closed-label
classification with genuinely ambiguous items (ChaosNLI, 100 human labels per item). Every
elicited arm is run under three elicitation protocols and reported as a range. The result is
negative. Across 48 model $\times$ dataset $\times$ protocol cells, the best-case triple
(a logistic combination fitted on a held-out calibration split) does not outperform a single
verbalized confidence number (mean paired AUROC difference $-0.010$; the $95\%$ interval excludes
zero in favour of the triple in $2$ cells and against it in $6$), and the indeterminacy component
adds no information over truth and falsity alone (mean difference $+0.003$). Semantic entropy
outperforms every elicited signal on free-form generation ($+0.11$ to $+0.21$ AUROC), and at a
guaranteed selective risk of $0.10$ the triple yields lower achieved coverage than the scalar in
$23$ of $36$ cells. A post-hoc analysis on ChaosNLI shows that neither the elicited nor a
sample-constructed indeterminacy tracks human label disagreement better than a scalar. We
conclude that elicited three-component epistemic states do not carry predictive information
beyond a single elicited number, and that arguments built on the triple as a measurement
instrument lack predictive support. Prompts, raw generations, gradings, the sealed analysis plan
and analysis code accompany this submission.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A language model asked a question it cannot answer has, in principle, several distinguishable
predicaments. It may lack the information. The question may be vague. The question may be about
a future contingency. The proposition may be paradoxical. A single scalar confidence score
compresses all of these into one number, and a normalized output distribution forces whatever
mass is withheld from one answer onto the others.

This observation has motivated a family of proposals that represent the model's state with a
triple rather than a scalar: degrees of truth, indeterminacy and falsity treated as separate and
not required to sum to one \citep{leyva2026breaking}. Empirically it is established that models
will emit such triples when asked, that the components frequently sum to more than one, and that
the phenomenon replicates across vendors \citep{mason2026tensors}. It is also established, by the
same replication, that the scalar triple is \emph{insufficient} to separate paradox from
ignorance from contingency: distinct predicaments can produce identical triples.

None of this answers the question a practitioner would ask. Suppose we accept that the triple is
a richer description. Does it \emph{do} anything? Concretely: if we want to know which of a
model's answers will turn out to be wrong, does the triple tell us more than simply asking the
model how confident it is?

That is the question this paper tests. We fix the target --- $Y=1$ if the model's answer is
incorrect --- and require every candidate signal to predict the same $Y$ on the same items with
the same evaluation protocol. This turns a representational claim into a measurable one.

\paragraph{Why the comparison must include verbalized scalar confidence.}
The decisive baseline is not semantic entropy. It is asking the model for one number. If a
three-component elicitation does not beat ``how confident are you, 0--100?'', then the additional
components are contributing vocabulary rather than information, and the representational argument
has no empirical support at the level of prediction. To our knowledge this comparison had not
been run. We treat it as the primary contrast and every other signal as a reference point.

\paragraph{Why protocol variation is part of the design, not a robustness check.}
Verbalized confidence is strongly sensitive to elicitation format
\citep{tian2023just,xiong2024can,protocolsens2026}. Any comparison between an elicited triple and
an elicited scalar that uses one prompt per arm is confounded by prompt choice. We therefore run
every elicited arm under three protocols that vary wording, scale and response format, and report
the full range.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A preregistered predictive-validity test of elicited three-component epistemic states
        against four baselines under a single fixed target and evaluation protocol, with the
        analysis plan and all deviations sealed in a version-controlled repository before
        collection (Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:prereg}).
  \item An explicit decisive contrast against verbalized scalar confidence, and an ablation
        isolating the contribution of the indeterminacy channel $I$ over $T$ and $F$ alone.
  \item Coverage of three task regimes --- free-form generation, closed-label classification
        with clean labels, and closed-label classification with human-measured ambiguity ---
        so that the indeterminacy component is tested against a target it could plausibly track.
  \item A decision-relevant evaluation: conformal abstention at a fixed target risk, reporting
        achieved coverage per signal, rather than discrimination metrics alone.
  \item A negative result, stated as such: the triple does not beat a single number, and $I$
        adds nothing, in every regime tested.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What we are not claiming.} We do not test whether three-component representations
are philosophically preferable, nor whether they describe internal states
\citep{mason2026observability}. We test one thing: whether the elicited numbers predict error.
The negative result does not refute the representational argument; it establishes that the
argument cannot currently be supported on predictive grounds.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Uncertainty quantification for LLMs.}
The dominant sampling-based signal is semantic entropy, which samples multiple generations and
measures dispersion over meaning-equivalence classes rather than surface strings
\citep{kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024detecting}. Self-verification approaches ask the model to
judge its own candidate answer, yielding $P(\text{True})$ \citep{kadavath2022know}.
Sequence-level token probability remains a strong and cheap baseline where log-probabilities are
exposed. Conformal prediction supplies distribution-free, finite-sample guarantees and is now
routinely combined with these scores to produce accept/abstain rules
\citep{angelopoulos2021gentle,acse2026}; selective prediction and the risk--coverage curve give
the decision-relevant framing \citep{geifman2017selective}.

\paragraph{Verbalized confidence and its fragility.}
A parallel line asks the model to state its confidence in words or numbers. Verbalized confidence
from RLHF-tuned models can be better calibrated than token probabilities in some settings
\citep{tian2023just}, but it concentrates in a narrow high band regardless of correctness, and the
reported number is governed by response scale and prompt structure
\citep{xiong2024can,protocolsens2026}. Probes on hidden states discriminate correctness better
than the verbal channel on the same items \citep{ahdritz2024distinguishing}. This literature is
the reason our design treats elicitation protocol as a factor.

\paragraph{Decomposed uncertainty.}
A growing literature asks whether a model's uncertainty can be split into components ---
epistemic versus aleatoric \citep{ahdritz2024distinguishing}, belief divergence versus belief
conflict \citep{shi2026notall} --- and whether models decline to answer when they should
\citep{know2guess2026}. The three-component triple tested here is one instantiation of this
idea; the question of whether such a decomposition can be \emph{elicited} verbally, and whether it
carries information beyond a scalar, is the gap this paper addresses.

\paragraph{Multi-component uncertainty formalisms.}
Representing belief with more than one number has several lineages: Dempster--Shafer belief
functions \citep{shafer1976mathematical}, subjective logic \citep{josang2016subjective}, imprecise
probability, and four-valued Belnap--Dunn logics, each with an established treatment of ignorance
and conflict. Neutrosophic $(T,I,F)$ belongs to this family and is the specific instantiation
tested here \citep{leyva2026breaking}. We take no position on the relations among these
formalisms; we evaluate one of them empirically, and the protocol applies unchanged to the others.

\paragraph{Positioning.} The closest prior work established that unconstrained elicitation
produces $T+I+F>1$ at high rates and that this replicates across vendors
\citep{leyva2026breaking,mason2026tensors}. Neither establishes predictive validity.

\section{What would count as evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

Let $x$ be a question, $\hat{a}(x)$ the model's answer under greedy decoding, and
$Y(x)=\mathbb{1}[\hat{a}(x)\text{ is incorrect}]$ under a fixed grading function. A \emph{signal}
is any map $s:x\mapsto\mathbb{R}$ intended to be monotone in the probability that $Y=1$. Every
signal in this study predicts the same $Y$, on the same items, from the same model, and is
evaluated with the same metrics on the same split.

For a triple $(T,I,F)$ we define three scalarizations, fixed in advance:
\begin{align}
s_{\mathrm{TF}} &= F - T, \label{eq:stf}\\
s_{\mathrm{TIF}} &= F + \tfrac{1}{2} I - T, \label{eq:stif}\\
s_{\mathrm{TIF}}^{\mathrm{lr}} &= \text{logistic combination of } (T,I,F) \text{ fitted on the calibration split.} \label{eq:slr}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:stf} ignores $I$ entirely; \eqref{eq:stif} charges indeterminacy at a fixed
rate; \eqref{eq:slr} lets the data choose the weights and is the most favourable treatment of the
triple. The contrast $s_{\mathrm{TIF}}^{\mathrm{lr}}$ versus $s_{\mathrm{TF}}$ isolates whether
$I$ carries information at all. Fitting \eqref{eq:slr} uses only the calibration split; all
reported metrics are computed on the held-out test split.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Signals}
\begin{description}
  \item[A. Elicited triple.] One additional call per item requesting $T$, $I$, $F$ in $[0,1]$
        with no normalization constraint, parsed to a triple. Scalarized by
        \eqref{eq:stf}--\eqref{eq:slr}.
  \item[B. Verbalized scalar confidence.] One additional call requesting a single integer
        $0$--$100$ (or a verbal grade under P3). \emph{Primary baseline.}
  \item[C. Self-verification $P(\text{True})$.] One additional call presenting the question and
        the model's own answer and asking whether it is true (binary under P1--P2; a stated
        probability under P3).
  \item[D. Sequence log-probability.] Mean token log-probability of the greedy answer where the
        provider exposes it: available for all items of gpt-4o-mini, $30\%$ of items of
        Llama-3.1-8B (provider-dependent), and none for Claude or Gemini.
  \item[E. Discrete semantic entropy.] $K=10$ samples at temperature $1.0$, normalized and
        clustered into equivalence classes by answer normalization, then Shannon entropy over
        cluster frequencies. On closed-label datasets the classes are the labels themselves.
\end{description}
Arms A, B and C are elicited and therefore run under all three protocols. Arms D and E are
sampling-based and have no elicitation protocol.

\subsection{Elicitation protocols}
Three protocols per elicited arm, varying wording, scale and response format simultaneously so
that they are distinguishable formats rather than paraphrases: P1, terse numeric, no explanation;
P2, explanation first, then numbers; P3, verbal grades (NONE $\ldots$ TOTAL) mapped to a fixed
numeric grid, with the dimensions presented in reverse order relative to P1. Full texts in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
Four datasets, $N=300$ items each, sampled with seed 20260823 and split $50/50$ into calibration
and test:
\begin{description}
  \item[TriviaQA] rc.nocontext validation \citep{joshi2017triviaqa}; free-form, rich alias sets.
  \item[NQ-open] validation split \citep{kwiatkowski2019natural,lee2019latent}; free-form, harder.
  \item[SciQ-MC] SciQ validation \citep{welbl2017sciq} served as four-option multiple choice
        using the dataset's own distractors, options shuffled with the fixed seed, the model
        answering with a letter. This is the closed-label regime with clean labels.
  \item[ChaosNLI] SNLI and MNLI-matched items with 100 human labels each
        \citep{nie2020chaosnli}; three labels; gold = majority label. Half of the sample is drawn
        from the lowest-entropy third of the pool (clear items) and half from the highest-entropy
        third (genuinely ambiguous items), so that the indeterminacy channel can be checked
        against measured human disagreement. This is the closed-label regime with ambiguous labels.
\end{description}
Short-form and closed-label tasks are a deliberate restriction: they make $Y$ well defined
without an LLM grader.

\subsection{Models}
Four models from four vendors, accessed through OpenRouter on 1~September~2026 with
temperature $0$ for greedy and elicited calls: \texttt{openai/gpt-4o-mini},
\texttt{anthropic/claude-haiku-4.5}, \texttt{google/gemini-2.5-flash},
\texttt{meta-llama/llama-3.1-8b-instruct}.

\subsection{Grading}
$Y$ is computed by normalized exact match against the gold answer set (lower-case, strip articles
and punctuation, alias expansion; letter or option text for SciQ-MC; label word for ChaosNLI).
Items where the model refuses to answer (\texttt{I DON'T KNOW}) are recorded separately and
excluded from the discrimination analysis: a signal cannot be credited for predicting the error of
an answer that was never given. Refusal rates: TriviaQA $4.2\%$, NQ-open $16.3\%$, SciQ-MC
$0.8\%$, ChaosNLI $0\%$; by model, $4.3$--$5.9\%$.

\subsection{Cost}
The realized design comprised $4{,}800$ valid item-cells and approximately $106{,}000$ API
invocations, of which about $2{,}700$ failed during a credit exhaustion in the first collection
pass and were re-collected in full in a second pass (the pipeline is resumable and re-collects
error rows; no partial records were used). Total spend was approximately US\$33.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Discrimination.} AUROC and AUPRC for predicting $Y=1$ on the test split.
\paragraph{Calibration.} Brier score and expected calibration error after isotonic mapping fitted
on the calibration split only.
\paragraph{Selective prediction.} Risk--coverage curve, its area, and accuracy at coverage
$\{0.9,0.8,0.5\}$ \citep{geifman2017selective}.
\paragraph{Conformal abstention.} For each signal, a threshold calibrated on the calibration
split to selective risk $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.10\}$; we report \emph{achieved} risk and
\emph{achieved} coverage on the test split.
\paragraph{Uncertainty.} Paired bootstrap over items, $B=10{,}000$, for every pairwise
difference; we report the difference and its $95\%$ interval, never two marginal intervals
\citep{miller2024adding}.
\paragraph{Protocol range.} Each elicited signal is reported over the three protocols, with a
variance decomposition of AUROC into signal, protocol, model and dataset components.

\section{Preregistered hypotheses and decision rules}
\label{sec:prereg}

Fixed before data collection and sealed with git tags (\texttt{prereg-2026-09-01}, \texttt{-b},
\texttt{-c}). Deviations are listed in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (primary).] $s_{\mathrm{TIF}}^{\mathrm{lr}}$ achieves higher AUROC than verbalized
        scalar confidence. \emph{Retain the triple only if} the paired bootstrap $95\%$ interval
        excludes zero under all three protocols and in a majority of model $\times$ dataset cells.
  \item[H2 (ablation).] $s_{\mathrm{TIF}}^{\mathrm{lr}}$ achieves higher AUROC than
        $s_{\mathrm{TF}}$. If the interval includes zero, the $I$ channel adds no predictive
        information, whatever H1 shows.
  \item[H3 (reference).] Semantic entropy achieves higher AUROC than all elicited arms.
  \item[H4 (protocol).] The between-protocol range of AUROC for a given elicited signal is
        smaller than the between-signal difference at fixed protocol.
  \item[H5 (decision).] At guaranteed selective risk $\alpha=0.10$, the triple yields higher
        achieved coverage than the scalar.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Reporting commitment.} All five hypotheses are reported with effect sizes and
intervals regardless of direction. The plan stated that if H1 and H2 both fail, the abstract
would say so. Both fail; the abstract says so.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Data quality.} Parse rates for the three preregistered elicited arms were at or
above $0.90$ in $141$ of $144$ arm $\times$ cell combinations; the three exceptions
($P(\text{True})$ P3 on Claude/ChaosNLI $0.83$ and Llama/SciQ-MC $0.88$; scalar P2 on

fixed in the plan for three of four models: its AUROC estimates are unstable and we report them
with that caveat rather than dropping the regime.

\paragraph{H1: the triple does not beat one number.} Table~\ref{tab:contrast} gives the
preregistered contrasts. The mean paired difference in AUROC between the fitted triple and the
verbalized scalar is $-0.011$, $-0.011$ and $-0.010$ under P1, P2 and P3. The $95\%$ interval
excludes zero in favour of the triple in $2$ of $48$ cells and against it in $6$; the difference
is positive in exactly $50\%$ of cells. The retention rule (interval excluding zero under all
three protocols, majority of cells) is not met under any protocol. By regime: TriviaQA
$+0.005$, NQ-open $-0.041$ (four cells significantly against the triple), SciQ-MC $-0.033$,
ChaosNLI $+0.028$ (one cell significantly in favour). Excluding SciQ-MC, whose base error rate
falls below the preregistered floor, the pooled difference is $-0.003$ over $36$ cells. The
between-cell standard error of the pooled mean is $0.011$, so an average advantage of $0.03$
AUROC --- a third of what semantic entropy delivers --- would have been detected. \textbf{H1 fails.}

\paragraph{H2: the indeterminacy channel adds nothing.} The fitted triple versus
$s_{\mathrm{TF}}$ differs by $-0.007$, $-0.003$ and $+0.019$ (P1--P3). Under P1 and P2 no cell
favours the triple significantly; under P3 four of sixteen cells do, all on the closed-label
datasets. Over all four regimes the mean difference is $+0.003$ (between-cell standard error
$0.007$). The preregistered rule asks whether the interval includes zero: it does under P1 and P2,
and the P3 gain is small and does not transfer to the primary contrast, which fails under P3 as
well. \textbf{H2 fails.} We conjecture, without testing it here, that the verbal-grade format
of P3 spreads $I$ over more of its range (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}) and that the fitted
combination exploits this on three- and four-label tasks; we report it rather than explain it.

\paragraph{H3: sampling bounds elicitation.} Semantic entropy exceeds the fitted triple by
$+0.096$, $+0.043$ and $+0.097$ (P1--P3), positive in $75\%$ of cells; the interval excludes
zero in favour of semantic entropy in $22$ of $48$ cells and against it in $3$. The advantage is concentrated in free-form generation (TriviaQA $+0.11$, NQ-open
$+0.21$, significant in all $12$ NQ-open cells); on the closed-label datasets the two are
indistinguishable (SciQ-MC $-0.006$, ChaosNLI $0.000$), where ten samples over three or four
labels carry little information. Where available, sequence log-probability is the strongest
signal of all ($0.80$ pooled; $0.83$--$0.87$ on generation).

\paragraph{H4: protocol does not dominate signal, but it moves the numbers.} The marginal
$\eta^2$ of AUROC is $0.28$ for dataset, $0.17$ for signal, $0.024$ for protocol and $0.011$ for
model (Table~\ref{tab:variance}). The between-protocol component is an order of magnitude smaller
than the between-signal component, so the ranking of elicited signals is identifiable and
\textbf{H4 holds}. Protocol nevertheless shifts the elicited values substantially without
changing their ranking. On ChaosNLI, the share of items with $I\geq0.5$ rises under P3 from
$1$--$3\%$ (P1--P2) to $27$--$63\%$ for Claude and Gemini; gpt-4o-mini sits at $30$--$39\%$
under every protocol because of the label collision described in Section~\ref{sec:posthoc},
and Llama at $13\%$. The fitted triple's AUROC moves between protocols by up to $0.10$ within a
model on ChaosNLI and by up to $0.22$ elsewhere (Gemini on NQ-open). The verbal-grade format
makes models \emph{use} the indeterminacy channel without making it informative.

\paragraph{H5: no coverage advantage at guaranteed risk.} At $\alpha=0.10$, in the $36$ cells
where both signals admit a feasible threshold, achieved coverage of the fitted triple minus that
of the scalar averages $-0.026$ ($+0.013$ under P2, $-0.020$ under P1, $-0.071$ under P3); the
triple covers more in $8$ cells, less in $23$, and the same in $5$. \textbf{H5 fails.} Two
caveats apply to both signals equally: with $150$ calibration items the achieved test risk exceeds
the nominal $0.10$ in about half of the cells ($47\%$ for the triple, $56\%$ for the scalar), and
semantic entropy gains $+0.11$ coverage over the scalar on the same cells ($18$ up, $12$ down).

\subsection{Post-hoc analyses (registered before collection, not part of the verdict)}
\label{sec:posthoc}

\paragraph{Label-semantics collision.} In the pilot, applying the arm-A prompt to ChaosNLI
produced triples that rated the \emph{meaning of the label} rather than the correctness of the
choice: an answer of ``contradiction'' yielded $(T\approx0, F\approx0.9)$, ``neutral'' yielded
$I\approx0.5$--$0.7$, and ``entailment'' yielded $T\approx1$, item after item. We kept the
preregistered prompt and added, on the two closed-label datasets only, a variant that names the
object of the rating explicitly (``rate the correctness of the choice, not the meaning of the
label''). The disambiguated triple is \emph{worse}: against the scalar, $-0.102$, $-0.023$ and
$-0.083$ (P1--P3); against the original triple, $-0.107$, $-0.016$ and $-0.066$. Telling the
model what to rate does not remove the collision and removes what little signal the format had.

\paragraph{Does any indeterminacy track human disagreement?} ChaosNLI provides, for each item,
the entropy of 100 human labels --- a target other than error that an indeterminacy component
could plausibly track. Table~\ref{tab:human} reports Spearman correlations. The elicited $I$
correlates with human entropy at $0.31$--$0.36$ for Claude (P1--P2), $0.06$--$0.21$ for Gemini,
$0.07$--$0.10$ for Llama, and \emph{negatively} for gpt-4o-mini ($-0.18$ under P2--P3, the
label collision again). Where either verbal signal tracks human entropy appreciably, the scalar does so at least as

\paragraph{Effective dimensionality.} Under P1 and P2 the share of items with $I>T$ is
$0$--$16\%$ in every cell except gpt-4o-mini on ChaosNLI ($41$--$47\%$, driven by the label

freedom dressed as three.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The plan drafted two discussion branches before the data existed. The preregistered tests select
the negative branch; we reproduce it here, and mark separately what rests on post-hoc evidence.

\paragraph{Elicited triples do not predict error better than a single elicited number.} This
held in free-form generation, in closed-label classification with clean labels, and in
closed-label classification with genuinely ambiguous items, on four models from four vendors,
under three elicitation formats. The reported prevalence of $T+I+F>1$ in earlier work
\citep{leyva2026breaking,mason2026tensors} therefore describes a property of unconstrained
elicitation rather than a usable signal, and any downstream argument that builds on the elicited
triple as a measurement instrument lacks predictive support.

\paragraph{The indeterminacy channel adds no predictive information.} This is the preregistered
result H2: $I$ adds nothing to $T$ and $F$ for predicting error under P1 and P2, and a small
protocol-specific gain under P3 ($+0.019$, four cells) that does not carry to the primary contrast.
The post-hoc ChaosNLI analysis (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}), which was registered before
collection but is not part of the verdict, points the same way: $I$ does not track human ambiguity
better than a scalar confidence, and a triple constructed from samples is the sample entropy under
another name. We state the post-hoc part as consistent with H2, not as established by it.

\paragraph{What elicitation is good for.} The verbal arms are not noise: they discriminate at
$0.60$--$0.80$ and, in one model, the verbal channel tracks item ambiguity better than the model's
own sampling ($0.42$ versus $0.18$). But the scalar does this as well as the triple. Verbalized
reports remain a legitimate object of study as \emph{behaviour} --- what a model says under a
protocol --- and a poor instrument for what it knows, consistent with the observability argument
of \citet{mason2026observability}.

\paragraph{Where a multi-component representation might still earn its place.} Not in
self-report. Three routes remain open and are not tested here: components read from internal
representations rather than asked for; components assigned by an analyst aggregating several
models or sources, where independence of support and conflict is a modelling choice rather than a
claim about introspection; and targets that are themselves typed, provided the type labels come
from outside the model. The last route is the one ChaosNLI approximates, and on it the elicited
$I$ did not deliver.

\section{Limitations and deviations}
\label{sec:limits}

$N=300$ per dataset rather than the planned $1{,}000$, for cost. Per cell ($150$ test items)
the paired-bootstrap intervals on an AUROC difference have a mean half-width of $0.11$ (H1) and
$0.04$ (H2), so single cells are underpowered for small effects; the study's power comes from
pooling $48$ cells, where the standard errors of the mean differences are $0.011$ (H1) and
$0.007$ (H2). A true average advantage of $0.03$ AUROC for the triple would have been detected;
advantages below that are not excluded and would not, in our judgement, justify three numbers over
one. Refusal and error rates per cell are given in Appendix~\ref{app:cells}. Short-form and closed-label tasks only; results may not transfer to long-form
generation. Discrete semantic entropy over normalized strings is weaker than NLI-based
clustering and is uninformative on closed-label tasks. Sequence log-probabilities were available
for one model in full and one in part. Three protocols is a small sample from a large space; the
protocol range is a lower bound on protocol sensitivity. Elicitation is behavioural: nothing here
licenses claims about internal representations. SciQ-MC's base error rate fell below the
preregistered $5\%$ floor.

Deviations from the sealed plan, all recorded before collection: (i) dataset loader rewritten to
read parquet files directly; (ii) \texttt{gemini-2.0-flash-001} replaced by
\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} (no longer served); (iii) items collected in parallel threads; (iv)
ChaosNLI added as a fourth dataset with a registered post-hoc analysis of $I$ against human
entropy; (v) SciQ served as multiple choice after the pilot showed its free-form gold answers
(cloze fragments) to be ungradable by exact match; (vi) a label-disambiguated triple added on the
closed-label datasets after the pilot exposed the label-semantics collision, reported separately
and excluded from the verdict; (vii) $N$ reduced to $300$; (viii) SciQ-MC retained although its
base error rate ($0.037$--$0.067$ by model) fell below the $5\%$ floor at which the plan said a
dataset should be dropped --- this is the one deviation recorded after collection, because the
rate is only known once the greedy answers exist; we keep the regime, flag its AUROC estimates as
unstable, and report the pooled contrasts with and without it (H1: $-0.010$ versus $-0.003$; H2:
$+0.003$ versus $-0.001$; H3: $+0.079$ versus $+0.107$), which do not change any verdict.
Deviations (iv) and (vi) were expected to favour the triple; (vi) did the opposite. None of
(i)--(vii) was made after seeing test-split results.

\section{Broader impact}
The practical use of any of these signals is to decide when a system should abstain. A signal
that looks richer than it is invites over-trust in exactly the settings --- medical, legal,
financial --- where abstention matters most. Our result argues against deploying elicited
multi-component states as abstention criteria on the strength of their form, and for the cheaper
and better-supported alternatives (sampling, log-probabilities) where they are available. We see
no direct risk from the release of prompts and generations; the datasets are public and contain
no personal data.

\section{Conclusion}
Under a preregistered decision rule, on four models and four datasets spanning generation and
classification, an elicited three-component epistemic state does not predict error better than a
single verbalized confidence number (H1), its indeterminacy component adds no predictive
information over truth and falsity (H2), and sampling-based signals dominate all elicited ones on
free-form generation (H3). The post-hoc evidence is consistent: as elicited, the triple behaves
as one degree of freedom. Claims that rest on the elicited triple as a measurement of what a
model knows should be read as claims about what the model says.

\section{Elicitation prompts}
\label{app:prompts}
All prompts are reproduced verbatim from \texttt{prompts.py} (version \texttt{p2-v1.1}).
Placeholders \texttt{\{question\}} and \texttt{\{answer\}} are filled with the item and the
model's greedy answer.

\paragraph{Answer call (all arms).} System: \emph{You are answering short factual questions.
Reply with the answer only: no preamble, no explanation, no punctuation beyond what the answer
needs. If you do not know, reply exactly: I DON'T KNOW.} User: \emph{Question: \{question\}
Answer:}

\paragraph{Arm A, P1.} System: \emph{You report epistemic states as three independent numbers.}
User: \emph{Question: \{question\} / Proposed answer: \{answer\} / Rate three independent degrees,
each in [0,1]. They need not sum to 1. T = degree to which the proposed answer is true; I = degree
of indeterminacy (vagueness, missing information, undecidability); F = degree to which the
proposed answer is false. Reply with exactly one line in this format and nothing else:
T=<number> I=<number> F=<number>}

\paragraph{Arm A, P2.} Same system. User: \emph{Question: \{question\} / Proposed answer:
\{answer\} / First, in at most three sentences, state what you actually know about this question,
what is uncertain, and why. Then, on a final separate line, give three independent degrees in
[0,1] which need not sum to 1: T (truth of the proposed answer), I (indeterminacy), F (falsity).
The final line must have exactly this format: T=<number> I=<number> F=<number>}

\paragraph{Arm A, P3.} System: \emph{You report epistemic states using verbal grades.} User:
\emph{Proposed answer: \{answer\} / Question: \{question\} / Assign one verbal grade to each of
the three dimensions below. The dimensions are independent: a high grade on one does not lower
the others. Scale: NONE / VERY LOW / LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH / VERY HIGH / TOTAL. FALSITY -- the
proposed answer is wrong; INDETERMINACY -- the matter is vague, unsettled or unknowable; TRUTH --
the proposed answer is right. Reply with exactly three lines and nothing else: FALSITY=<grade>
INDETERMINACY=<grade> TRUTH=<grade>} Grid: NONE 0, VERY LOW 0.15, LOW 0.3, MEDIUM 0.5, HIGH 0.7,
VERY HIGH 0.85, TOTAL 1.

\paragraph{Arm B, P1.} System: \emph{You report confidence as a single number.} User:
\emph{Question: \{question\} / Proposed answer: \{answer\} / How confident are you that the
proposed answer is correct? Reply with a single integer from 0 to 100 and nothing else.}

\paragraph{Arm B, P2.} Same system. User: \emph{$\ldots$ First, in at most three sentences,
state what you actually know about this question, what is uncertain, and why. Then, on a final
separate line, give your confidence that the proposed answer is correct as an integer from 0 to
100, in exactly this format: CONFIDENCE=<integer>}

\paragraph{Arm B, P3.} System: \emph{You report confidence using verbal grades.} User:
\emph{Proposed answer: \{answer\} / Question: \{question\} / How likely is it that the proposed
answer is CORRECT? Scale: NONE / VERY LOW / LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH / VERY HIGH / TOTAL. Reply with
exactly one line and nothing else: CORRECTNESS=<grade>}

\paragraph{Arm C, P1.} System: \emph{You verify proposed answers.} User: \emph{Question:
\{question\} / Proposed answer: \{answer\} / Is the proposed answer true? Reply with a single
word, True or False, and nothing else.}

\paragraph{Arm C, P2.} Same system. User: \emph{$\ldots$ Check the proposed answer in at most
three sentences, then give your verdict on a final separate line in exactly this format:
VERDICT=<True or False>}

\paragraph{Arm C, P3.} Same system. User: \emph{Proposed answer: \{answer\} / Question:
\{question\} / What is the probability that the proposed answer is TRUE? Reply with a single
number between 0 and 1 and nothing else.}

\paragraph{Arm A-label (post hoc, closed-label datasets).} As arm A, with ``Task:'' and
``Chosen label:'' in place of ``Question:'' and ``Proposed answer:'', and the sentence \emph{The
chosen label is one option from a fixed set of classification labels. Rate the CORRECTNESS OF
THE CHOICE, not the meaning of the label itself}, with $T$/$I$/$F$ redefined as the degree to
which the chosen label is the correct classification / indeterminacy about which label is correct
/ the degree to which the chosen label is the wrong classification.
